# Attorney Advertising Compliance Reference

Read this whenever a `bar_rule` finding fires. Marketing copy gets firms in
trouble here far more often than it does on substantive legal accuracy, and
unlike a stale statute, these are violations the firm commits on purpose because
the language tests well.

## 1. The rules as they actually read today

**The single most important fact for any legal-marketing checklist: Model Rules
7.4 and 7.5 were DELETED in August 2018** (ABA House of Delegates Resolution 101).
Their substance moved into Rule 7.2(c) and the Comments to 7.1 and 7.2. Live
Model Rules are **7.1, 7.2, 7.3, 7.6**. Any checklist citing "Model Rule 7.4
(specialization)" or "Model Rule 7.5 (firm names)" as live authority is
describing the pre-2018 regime.

**But states did not follow uniformly**, and this is where most errors happen:

- **California did NOT adopt the consolidation.** CA retains Rule 7.4
  (specialization) and Rule 7.5 (firm names) as separate rules. In California,
  "7.4 and 7.5 no longer exist" is wrong.
- **Pennsylvania adopted it in 2024** — 7.4, 7.5, and 7.7 removed and reserved.
- **Georgia adopted it**, with 7.4 and 7.5 Reserved, amended effective 3/1/2026.

**Rule 7.1** — a lawyer shall not make a false or misleading communication.
Comment [3] carries the contingency-fee rule; Comment [4] the
unjustified-expectation and unsubstantiated-comparison rule; Comment [6] trade
names. **Rule 7.2** — advertising, payment for recommendations, specialization
claims, and the responsible-lawyer contact requirement. **Rule 7.3** —
solicitation.

## 2. Phrase trigger table

### 2.1 Guarantees and predictions — highest risk

**Triggers:** "we will win," "guaranteed compensation," "you will receive,"
"guaranteed results," "100% success," "we'll get you the maximum," "risk-free,"
"we guarantee."

**Authority:** Model Rule 7.1 + Cmt [4]. California is explicit and per se —
Rule 7.1 Cmt [2]: *"A communication that contains an express guarantee or
warranty of the result of a particular representation is a false or misleading
communication under this rule."* Florida 4-7.13(b)(1) bans anything "a
prospective client can reasonably interpret as a prediction or guaranty of
success or specific results." California SB 37 (statutory, eff. 1/1/2026) bans
"a guarantee or warranty of success regarding the outcome of a legal matter."

**No disclaimer cures an express guarantee.** This is a rewrite, not a footnote —
the one category where adding fine print does not help. Replace with what the
firm actually does: "we pursue the maximum compensation available under the
applicable policy limits."

### 2.2 Superlatives and comparisons

**Triggers:** best, top, #1, most experienced, leading, premier, most successful,
toughest, unmatched, top-rated, award-winning (unqualified).

**Authority:** Model Rule 7.1 Cmt [4] — unsubstantiated comparisons mislead "if
presented with such specificity as would lead a reasonable person to conclude
that the comparison or claim can be substantiated." Florida 4-7.13(b)(3) bans
characterizations of skills, experience, reputation, or record "unless the
characterization is objectively verifiable." Texas 7.02. **New Jersey RPC
7.1(a)(3) requires all three**: the comparing organization named, a substantiable
basis, **and** the verbatim NJ disclaimer. Louisiana 7.2(c)(1). California SB 37.

**Awards and ratings** ("Super Lawyers," "Best Lawyers," "Top 100"): Florida
4-7.14(a)(3) — the conferring entity must be "generally recognized within the
legal profession as being a bona fide organization that makes its selections
based on objective and uniformly applied criteria." Practice: state the **exact
award name, the organization, and the year.** New Jersey allows only "included in
the list with that name" — no self-describing as "super" or "the best."
**California SB 37 newly bans award references** unless the award is not
conferred by virtue of membership and the organization does not charge or solicit
a fee for it.

**The fix is almost always the same:** convert an unverifiable superlative into a
verifiable fact. "Best truck accident lawyer in Houston" → "has handled more than
400 commercial trucking cases since 2009."

### 2.3 Specialist / expert / certified — three distinct tiers

| Claim | Model Rules | Strict states |
|---|---|---|
| "We handle X" / "practice limited to X" | Always fine if true | Fine |
| "specializes in X" / "specialist" / "expert" | Permitted if substantiable by experience or training (7.2 Cmt [9]) | **Prohibited or disclaimer-conditioned** in TX, PA, NJ, CA |
| "Board Certified" / "certified specialist" | Only if certified by a state-approved or ABA-accredited org **and** the org is named (7.2(c)) | Same, plus state wording |

- **Texas** — permitted forms only: "Board Certified, [area] — Texas Board of
  Legal Specialization," or "Certified [area] [certifying organization]" where
  the org is Texas-accredited. Otherwise a lawyer "shall not advertise... that
  the lawyer is a specialist."
- **Pennsylvania** — 7.2(c)(3)(i): an experience-based specialty claim "shall
  include a disclaimer stating that the lawyer is not certified in the claimed
  specialty." 7.2(c)(3)(ii): **"a lawyer may not claim specialization in more
  than one field of law"** — unique in the U.S.
- **Florida** — 4-7.14(a)(5): allowed if certified under the Florida plan or an
  accredited plan, **or** objectively verifiable from education, training,
  experience, or substantial involvement. 4-7.14(a)(6): a firm-level claim that
  can't be verified for every lawyer needs a clear and conspicuous disclaimer
  that not all lawyers in the firm specialize.
- **New Jersey** — RPC 7.4: no specialization claim except NJ Supreme Court
  certification, ABA-approved certification, USPTO patent practice, or admiralty.
- **Ohio** — liberalized in 2024; certifying organizations beyond the state
  commission may certify.

### 2.4 Past results

**Triggers:** any settlement or verdict figure, "recovered over $X," results
carousels, "our results speak for themselves."

- **New York — the only mandated verbatim sentence in the country.** 22 NYCRR
  1200.7.1(e)(3): results claims are permitted "provided that it is accompanied
  by the following disclaimer: **'Prior results do not guarantee a similar
  outcome'**." Also requires factual support as of the publication date, and
  written informed consent for a testimonial about a pending matter.
- **Texas** — 7.02 is the most demanding substantively: the communicating lawyer
  or a firm member must have served as **lead counsel**, the amount stated must
  be what the client **actually received**, and the communication must disclose
  the nature of the case and the damages or injuries. **If a gross amount is
  stated, attorney fees and expenses must be stated too.**
- **Florida** — 4-7.13(b)(2): banned unless "objectively verifiable," and must
  not omit facts that make the result misleading.
- **Louisiana** — 7.2(c)(1)(D) requires "a disclaimer **such as** 'Results May
  Vary' or 'Past Results are not a Guarantee of Future Success'." Because the
  rule says "such as," Louisiana prescribes content, **not exact wording** — do
  not present either string as compelled text.
- **Alabama** (eff. 1/1/2026) — results must be objectively verifiable.

### 2.5 Testimonials and endorsements

- **Florida 4-7.13(b)(9)** — a testimonial is deceptive if it: (A) covers matters
  the person is unqualified to evaluate; (B) is not the person's actual
  experience; (C) does not represent what the firm's clients generally experience;
  (D) **was written or drafted by the lawyer**; (E) was given in exchange for
  something of value; or (F) omits a disclaimer that the prospective client may
  not obtain the same or similar results.
- **New York 1200.7.1(c)(1)** — no paid endorsement or testimonial "without
  disclosing that the person is being compensated therefor."
- **Pennsylvania 7.2(e)** — paid endorsements must be disclosed, and: **"No
  advertisement or public communication shall contain an endorsement by a
  celebrity or public figure."** Distinctive — PA flatly bans it.
- **Alabama** (2026) — testimonials require actual knowledge and personal
  experience; actors prohibited except in non-speaking background roles.

### 2.6 "No fee unless we win" — the costs-versus-fees distinction

**The most consistently enforced disclosure requirement in the country.**

- Model Rule 7.1 Cmt [3] — if the client may owe costs regardless of outcome, the
  communication "may not indicate that the lawyer's fee is contingent on
  obtaining a recovery unless the communication also discloses that the client
  may be responsible for court costs and expenses of litigation."
- **California** Rule 7.1 Cmt [3], verbatim: *"Any communication that states or
  implies 'no fee without recovery' is also misleading unless the communication
  also expressly discloses whether or not the client will be liable for costs."*
- **Pennsylvania** 7.2(g)(1); **Florida** 4-7.14(a)(7) (must disclose all fees
  and expenses the client might be liable for); **Texas** 7.04 (must state
  whether the client owes court costs, and whether the percentage is computed
  before or after expenses).
- **New York** — contingent rates may be advertised only with the 1200.7.1(p)
  information, and all fee advertising must comply with Judiciary Law § 488(3).

**Fee-honoring periods, frequently missed:** PA and FL — an advertised specific
fee, hourly rate, or range must be honored **90 days** (1 year for annual media).
Texas — **more than one year** after publication. New York — 30 days / until the
next issue / minimum 90 days depending on publication frequency, and a lawyer may
not charge more than an advertised range or hourly rate.

### 2.7 Jurisdictional disclosure — the biggest multi-market risk

A national landing page listing cities where the firm has no bona fide office
implicates PA 7.2(h), TX 7.04, LA 7.2, MO 4-7.2(e), and CA SB 37 simultaneously.

- **Pennsylvania 7.2(h)** — must disclose the geographic location by city or town
  of the office where the lawyers who will actually perform the services
  principally practice; county if outside the city or town.
- **Texas 7.04** — principal office by city or town. Satellite offices require a
  lawyer present three days weekly or an appointment-only disclosure. **If the
  lawyer knows or should know a case will likely be referred out, "a statement of
  such fact shall be conspicuously included."**
- **Missouri 4-7.2(e)** — may not advertise a non-principal office unless staffed
  by a lawyer three days a week, or the ad states the days and times or that
  meetings are by appointment only.
- **Louisiana 7.2** — bona fide office by city or town, **the date of admission
  to the LSBA and any other bars**, and an **LSBA-assigned filing number in the
  ad itself** (unique to Louisiana).
- **California SB 37** — the name of at least one California-licensed lawyer or
  firm responsible for the ad, **and** the city, town, or county of at least one
  bona fide office or the State Bar address of record.
- **Alabama** (2026) — must disclose when **not** licensed in Alabama.

### 2.8 "Attorney Advertising" labeling

The Model Rules **dropped** this in 2018. This is a per-state lookup, not a
general rule, and over-labeling is its own error.

| State | Label | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **NY** | "Attorney Advertising" | 1200.7.1(f) — first page, or the website **home page** (not a footer). Email: **subject line** must contain "ATTORNEY ADVERTISING." Exempt: radio, TV, billboard, directory, periodicals. |
| **TX** | "ADVERTISEMENT" | 7.05 — non-electronic written solicitation, first page and envelope face, ≥ 3/8" or 3× body height, sharply contrasting color. Email: subject line. |
| **CA** | "Advertisement" | 7.3(c) — written/recorded/electronic solicitation to a person known to need legal services; outside envelope and at start and end of recordings. |
| **NJ** | "ADVERTISEMENT" | RPC 7.3(b)(5)(i) — capitals, top of first page and on the envelope, for unsolicited contact about a specific event. |
| **AL** | "ADVERTISEMENT" | eff. 1/1/2026 — solicitation letters and emails, in a contrasting color. Short-form internet ads follow a "one-click rule." |

### 2.9 Trade names

Model Rule 7.1 Cmt [6]: permitted if not misleading. Distinctive **website
addresses and social media usernames are expressly blessed**. Misleading if it
implies a connection with a government agency or a public or charitable legal
services organization — a name like "Springfield Legal Clinic" may need an
express statement that it is not legal aid. **Texas Rule 7.01 bars trade names
outright in private practice.** New York 1200.7.1(c)(2) bans fictitious firm
portrayals; Kentucky, New Jersey, and Rhode Island retain stricter restrictions.

**Relevant to SEO work specifically:** New York 1200.7.1(g) bans "meta tags or
other hidden computer codes that, if displayed, would violate these Rules."

## 3. Mandatory verbatim strings — the only compelled text

Insert these exactly. Everything else on this page is prescribed in **substance**,
so generate compliant wording rather than quoting.

| String | State | Trigger | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| `Prior results do not guarantee a similar outcome` | **NY** | Results claims, comparisons, testimonials, quality characterizations (7.1(d)-(e)) | verified verbatim |
| `Attorney Advertising` (email subject: `ATTORNEY ADVERTISING`) | **NY** | 1200.7.1(f); website home page | verified verbatim |
| `The choice of a lawyer is an important decision and should not be based solely upon advertisements.` | **MO** | All ads except the narrow 4-7.2(g) safe harbor | verified verbatim from courts.mo.gov |
| `No aspect of this advertisement has been approved by the Supreme Court of New Jersey` | **NJ** | **Comparative claims only** — not a blanket NJ disclaimer. This is the precision point most checklists get wrong. | verify against njcourts.gov |
| `Before making your choice of attorney, you should give this matter careful thought. The selection of an attorney is an important decision.` | **NJ** | Bottom of the last page of solicitation letters | verify against njcourts.gov |
| `You arrived at this page via a paid advertisement on [search engine] through paid keyword search results. This website and the legal business it describes are affiliated only with [purchasing attorney] and the attorneys referenced within this website.` | **NJ** | Landing pages reached from a sponsored result purchased on a **competitor's name**. Must match the font size and color predominantly used on the page. | verified — the only new mandatory string added anywhere in 2025–2026 |
| `Board Certified, [area] — Texas Board of Legal Specialization` | **TX** | Certification claims | verified verbatim |

**Explicitly NOT compelled — do not quote as rule text:**

- **Louisiana** "Results May Vary" / "Past Results are not a Guarantee of Future
  Success" — the rule says "a disclaimer **such as**."
- **All Florida disclaimers.** Florida **deleted** its mandated wording effective
  **August 21, 2023**, including "Not Certified as a Specialist by The Florida
  Bar" and the direct-mail opener "If you have already retained a lawyer for this
  matter, please disregard this letter." The standard changed from "reasonably
  prominent" to "clear and conspicuous." **Any checklist still inserting those
  three Florida strings is wrong** — this is the most common source of stale
  Florida compliance advice.
- Pennsylvania's "not certified in the claimed specialty" and dramatization
  disclosures.
- Contingency-fee cost disclosures everywhere.
- **Georgia — nothing is verified.** The rules changed effective **March 1, 2026**
  (Supreme Court order S24U0172, approved 11/18/2025) and no official verbatim
  text was obtainable; gabar.org renders rule text via JavaScript and the order
  PDF is robots-blocked. Retrieve S24U0172 before drafting anything Georgia-specific.

## 4. State regimes

**Texas** — Part VII, Rules 7.01–7.07. **A filing state**: solicitations, public
media ads, and **website initial-access pages** must be filed with the Advertising
Review Committee. Optional binding pre-approval if filed ≥ 30 days ahead — a
compliance finding is binding in the lawyer's favor; a non-compliance finding is
not binding in a disciplinary proceeding. 4-year retention. Equal-prominence
clause: required disclosures "must be presented in the same manner as the
communication and with equal prominence." Distinctive: no false or misleading
motto, slogan, or jingle.

**Florida** — subchapter 4-7. **The strictest filing regime**: file at least **20
days before first use**; Bar reviews within 15 days. **Fees rose 7/1/2026 to $250
timely / $750 late.** "Any change of any kind to an advertisement renders the
advertisement a new advertisement with a new filing fee" — including wording,
photographs, layout, or color scheme. **Websites are exempt from filing** but
fully subject to 4-7.13 and 4-7.14.

**New York** — 22 NYCRR Part 1200. Two mandatory verbatim strings. Distinctive
structure: NY *permits* results statements, comparisons, testimonials, and quality
characterizations, but only if not misleading, factually supportable **as of the
publication date**, accompanied by the prior-results disclaimer, and (for pending
matters) with written informed consent. **7.1(k) retention** is unusual: website
contents preserved on initial publication, on any major redesign or meaningful
content change, and **in no event less than every 90 days.** No pre-filing.
A comprehensive rewrite aligning NY with the Model Rules was **proposed** in
December 2025 — **proposed only; the current rules remain in force.**

**California — the highest-priority state.** **SB 37** (Ch. 645, Stats. 2025)
became operative **January 1, 2026** and moved California from bar-discipline-only
exposure to **private civil litigation**: a private right of action with statutory
damages of **$5,000 to $100,000 per violation**, or three times actual damages,
whichever is larger, plus fees and injunctive relief; reported 72-hour removal
deadline after a State Bar complaint. Reported to reach websites, landing pages,
paid search, paid social, and lead-gen materials. Verify the damages figures and
the 72-hour rule against the chaptered text before relying on them. California
also retains Rules 7.4 and 7.5, and Rule 7.1(2) lets the Board of Trustees adopt
standards that are **presumed** to violate the rules — effectively a blacklist
enforced by burden-shifting.

**New Jersey** — **the most restrictive content rule in the country.** RPC 7.2(a):
"All advertisements shall be predominantly informational. **No drawings,
animations, dramatizations, music, or lyrics shall be used in connection with
televised advertising.**" No techniques that "depend upon absurdity." 30-day
blackout after a mass-disaster event. Reusing an ad the Committee on Attorney
Advertising has disapproved is itself unethical.

**Illinois** — Rule 7.2 amended effective **7/1/2025** with the country's most
detailed **Intermediary Connecting Service** regime — the key lead-generation rule
in the U.S. A lawyer may accept an ICS connection only after disclosing the
relationship and any connecting or comparative-advertising fee, and taking
reasonable steps to verify eight conditions, including that the ICS does not set
fees, give legal advice, solicit improperly, or refer or recommend lawyers, and
that its charges are not contingent on outcome or a percentage of the fee. Written
ICS certification creates a due-diligence safe harbor. **If the client uses a
lead-gen vendor, this rule is directly on point.**

**Pennsylvania** — adopted the ABA consolidation in 2024; 7.4, 7.5, 7.7 removed
and reserved; ad retention repealed. **7.2(j)** is distinctive and directly targets
ad-and-refer models: *"A lawyer or law firm shall not advertise as a pretext to
refer cases obtained from advertising to other lawyers."* No filing requirement.

**Louisiana** — filing required prior to or concurrently with first dissemination;
the **LSBA filing number must appear in the ad itself**. "Specialist" is permitted
in Louisiana, subject to the false-and-misleading standard.

**Missouri** — one mandatory verbatim disclaimer with a narrow safe harbor. The
4-7.2(g) exemption covers only firm and lawyer names, fields of practice, bar
admission dates, and contact information and hours. **Adding results,
testimonials, or fee claims — including "free consultation" — arguably takes the
ad outside it.** Treat the exemption narrowly. Multilingual clause: required words
must appear in **each** language used.

**Georgia** — Rules 7.1–7.3 amended effective **3/1/2026**; 7.4 and 7.5 Reserved.
Reported: 2-year retention, responsible-lawyer name, a disclaimer where a site
contains contingent-fee or "no fee unless you win" language, equal prominence, no
filing requirement. **All Georgia specifics are unverified.**

**Alabama** — effective **1/1/2026**: pre-publication filing **abolished**,
replaced with 6-year retention; general disclaimer requirement replaced with
context-specific ones; "ADVERTISEMENT" in contrasting color on solicitations;
must disclose when not licensed in Alabama; actors prohibited except non-speaking
background roles; direct PI solicitation permitted **21+ days** after the incident.

## 5. 2024–2026 changes

| Jurisdiction | Change | Effective |
|---|---|---|
| **California** | **SB 37** — statutory disclosure mandates + **private right of action, $5,000–$100,000** | **1/1/2026** |
| **Illinois** | Rule 7.2 rewritten to regulate lead-gen (ICS) | 7/1/2025 |
| **New Jersey** | ACPE Op. 735 Supplement — competitive keyword ads permitted **with a new mandatory landing-page disclaimer** | notice 12/8/2025 |
| **Georgia** | Rules 7.1–7.3 amended | **3/1/2026** |
| **Alabama** | Rules rewritten; filing abolished, 6-year retention | **1/1/2026** |
| **New York** | GBL § 396-b synthetic performer (AI) disclosure | **6/9/2026** |
| **Pennsylvania** | Adopted ABA consolidation | 11/2024 |
| **Florida** | Filing fees to $250 / $750; Online Ad Submission | **7/1/2026** |
| **Ohio** | Specialization certification liberalized | 2024 |
| **New York** | Comprehensive 7.x rewrite — **PROPOSED ONLY** | not adopted |

**Enforcement picture.** No public 2024–2026 disciplinary decision was found
sanctioning a firm specifically for SEO content, website copy, or AI-generated
marketing. The realistic mechanisms are: prospective screening in FL, TX, LA, and
NJ; and — the biggest change in the risk profile — **California SB 37 shifting
enforcement from bar discipline to private civil litigation.** Weight severity
accordingly: a California page carries a different kind of exposure than the same
page in a discipline-only state.

**Ethics opinions worth knowing:** ABA Formal Op. 501 (2022) — third-party
solicitation; **marketing firms hired by the lawyer** are the lawyer's agents and
remain restricted, and the lawyer must supervise. NYC Bar Formal Op. 2015-7 —
the leading LinkedIn analysis.

## 6. AI-generated content

Do not overstate this — only a handful of authorities address it, and vendor
blogs routinely exaggerate.

1. **Florida Ethics Opinion 24-1** (1/24/2024) is the clearest on-point authority.
   On advertising and intake specifically: the lawyer is "ultimately responsible
   should the chatbot provide misleading information to prospective clients or
   communicate in a manner that is inappropriately intrusive or coercive," and
   **"A lawyer must inform prospective clients that they are communicating with an
   AI program."** It also suggests screening questions to limit communication with
   already-represented persons.
2. **New York GBL § 396-b**, effective **June 9, 2026** — first in the nation.
   Requires a "conspicuous disclosure" when an ad includes an **AI-generated
   synthetic performer**. Penalties $1,000 first violation, $5,000 subsequent.
   Applies only with **actual knowledge**; excludes audio-only ads, expressive
   works, translation of human performers, and mere disseminators. This is general
   advertising law, not a bar rule, but it reaches law firm video and display ads
   using AI-generated people. It stacks on NY 1200.7.1(c)(3) and FL 4-7.13(b)(5)-(7).
3. **ABA Formal Opinion 512** (7/29/2024) covers generative AI — competence,
   confidentiality, client communication, fees. **It is silent on advertising.**

**Bottom line: outside Florida (chatbots) and New York (synthetic performers), no
state requires labeling AI-*written* marketing copy.** AI-generated text is
governed by ordinary Rule 7.1 accuracy and Rule 5.1/5.3 supervision — the firm
owns the accuracy of whatever the model produced, which is precisely the problem
this skill exists to catch.
